'''Phishing''' is a form of [[Social engineering (security)|social engineering]] and a [[scam]] in which attackers deceive people into revealing [[Information sensitivity|sensitive information]] or installing [[malware]]. Phishing can be used for [[Credential#Information technology|credential]] theft, [[session hijacking]], malware delivery, or [[Arbitrary code execution|command execution]]. Some attacks transparently relay [[authentication]] to a legitimate site, allowing the attacker to capture credentials and authenticated sessions. Phishing is a common initial-access method, and [[Large language model]]s can automate personalized phishing at low cost.

Common forms include general email phishing, targeted spear phishing and whaling, [[Voice phishing|voice phishing]] (vishing), and SMS phishing (smishing). Other forms use [[QR code]]s or relay authentication through an adversary-in-the-middle.

Measures to prevent or reduce the impact of phishing attacks include [[#Legal responses|legislation]], user education, public awareness, and technical security measures.

==Types==

===Email phishing===
Phishing attacks, often delivered via [[email]], attempt to trick individuals into giving away [[Information sensitivity|sensitive information]] or [[Credential#Information technology|login credentials]].<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Jansson |first1=K. |last2=von Solms |first2=R. |date=November 9, 2011 |title=Phishing for phishing awareness |url=http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0144929X.2011.632650 |journal=Behaviour & Information Technology |volume=32 |issue=6 |pages=584–593 |doi=10.1080/0144929X.2011.632650 |s2cid=5472217 |issn=0144-929X |url-access=subscription}}</ref> General phishing is sent broadly, whereas spear phishing targets a particular person or group.<ref name="Thomopoulos2024">{{cite journal |last1=Thomopoulos |first1=George A. |last2=Lyras |first2=Dimitrios P. |last3=Fidas |first3=Christos A. |title=A systematic review and research challenges on phishing cyberattacks from an electroencephalography and gaze-based perspective |journal=Personal and Ubiquitous Computing |volume=28 |year=2024 |pages=449–470 |doi=10.1007/s00779-024-01794-9 |doi-access=free}}</ref> The goal of the attacker can vary, with common targets including [[financial institution]]s, email and cloud productivity providers, and [[Streaming media|streaming services]].<ref name="2019cfs">{{cite journal |last1=Furnell |first1=Steven |last2=Millet |first2=Kieran |last3=Papadaki |first3=Maria |title=Fifteen years of phishing: can technology save us? |journal=Computer Fraud & Security |date=July 2019 |volume=2019 |issue=7 |pages=11–16 |doi=10.1016/S1361-3723(19)30074-0 |s2cid=199578115 |url=https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334551961 |url-access=subscription}}</ref> The stolen information or access may be used to steal money, install [[malware]], or spear phish others within the target organization.<ref name="the big phish">{{cite web |title=Spoofing and Phishing |url=https://www.fbi.gov/how-we-can-help-you/scams-and-safety/common-frauds-and-scams/spoofing-and-phishing |access-date=May 30, 2026 |website=[[Federal Bureau of Investigation]]}}</ref> Compromised streaming service accounts may also be sold on [[darknet market]]s.<ref name="atlanticnflx">{{cite web |last=Waddell |first=Kaveh |title=The Black Market for Netflix Accounts |url=https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2016/02/the-black-market-for-netflix-accounts/462321/ |website=The Atlantic |access-date=March 21, 2021 |date=February 11, 2016}}</ref> Such attacks can involve messages that appear to be from a trusted source, such as a bank or government agency. The messages may redirect to a [[Website spoofing|counterfeit login page]] that collects credentials.<ref name="Thomopoulos2024" />

{{anchor|spearphishing}}
===Spear phishing===

[[File:Spearphishing-Image.png|thumb|upright=1.3|alt=Flowchart showing operators using a spoofed Google email to send a malicious link and collect credentials from a target.|[[National Security Agency|NSA]] diagram of a [[GRU (Russian Federation)|GRU]] spear-phishing campaign]]

Spear phishing attacks can be more effective than general phishing attempts because they are tailored to specific individuals and use personal or organizational information to increase credibility.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Alsharnouby |first1=Mohamed |last2=Alaca |first2=Furkan |last3=Chiasson |first3=Sonia |title=Why phishing still works: User strategies for combating phishing attacks |journal=International Journal of Human-Computer Studies |volume=82 |date=2015 |doi=10.1016/j.ijhcs.2015.05.005 |pages=69–82 |url=https://linkinghub.elsevier.com/retrieve/pii/S1071581915000993 |access-date=May 6, 2026 |url-access=subscription}}</ref> Whaling is a form of targeted phishing directed at senior decision-makers with access to valuable information.<ref name="Thomopoulos2024" /> Automation can make personalized messages inexpensive enough to use at scale.<ref name="Czybik2026">{{cite conference |last1=Czybik |first1=Stefan |last2=Kouam |first2=Anne Josiane |last3=Heubl |first3=Peter |last4=Nold |first4=Jan Magnus |last5=Rieck |first5=Konrad |title=A Large-Scale Study of Personalized Phishing using Large Language Models |book-title=35th USENIX Security Symposium |publisher=[[USENIX|USENIX Association]] |year=2026 |pages=1687–1706 |isbn=978-1-939133-58-8 |url=https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity26/presentation/czybik |access-date=September 20, 2026}}</ref>

A field experiment sent simulated phishing emails to 100 younger and 58 older adults over 21 days. In this sample, 43% of participants clicked at least one simulated phishing link, and older women recorded the highest click rate among the four age-and-gender groups studied. Susceptibility declined among younger participants during the study but remained stable among older participants.<ref name="Lin">{{cite journal |last1=Lin |first1=Tian |last2=Capecci |first2=Daniel E. |last3=Ellis |first3=Donovan M. |last4=Rocha |first4=Harold A. |last5=Dommaraju |first5=Sandeep |last6=Oliveira |first6=Daniela S. |last7=Ebner |first7=Natalie C. |date=September 2019 |title=Susceptibility to Spear-Phishing Emails: Effects of Internet User Demographics and Email Content |journal=[[ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction]] |volume=26 |issue=5 |page=32 |doi=10.1145/3336141 |issn=1073-0516 |pmc=7274040 |pmid=32508486}}</ref>

The Russian government-run Threat Group-4127 ([[Fancy Bear]]; GRU Unit 26165) used spear phishing against targets associated with [[Hillary Clinton]]'s [[Hillary Clinton 2016 presidential campaign|2016 presidential campaign]] and the [[Democratic National Committee]].<ref>{{cite news |last1=Nakashima |first1=Ellen |last2=Harris |first2=Shane |date=July 13, 2018 |title=How the Russians hacked the DNC and passed its e‑mails to WikiLeaks |newspaper=[[The Washington Post]] |url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/national-security/how-the-russians-hacked-the-dnc-and-passed-its-emails-to-wikileaks/2018/07/13/af19a828-86c3-11e8-8553-a3ce89036c78_story.html?noredirect=on |url-status=live |access-date=February 22, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210321122509/https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/national-security/how-the-russians-hacked-the-dnc-and-passed-its-emails-to-wikileaks/2018/07/13/af19a828-86c3-11e8-8553-a3ce89036c78_story.html?noredirect=on |archive-date=March 21, 2021 }}</ref> SecureWorks linked the group to a separate 2015 campaign that targeted more than 1,800&nbsp;[[Google]] accounts and used the spoofed domain {{tt|accoounts-google.com}}.<ref>{{cite web |title=Threat Group-4127 targets Google accounts |date=June 26, 2016 |website=Secureworks |language=en |url=https://www.secureworks.com/research/threat-group-4127-targets-google-accounts |url-status=live |access-date=October 12, 2017 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190811223356/https://www.secureworks.com/research/threat-group-4127-targets-google-accounts |archive-date=August 11, 2019 }}</ref>

===Voice phishing (vishing)===
[[File:Ozonwoye Quick Reference Guide to Identify Vishing.png|thumb|upright=1.43|alt=Checklist of warning signs and response steps for a caller requesting passwords, account access, personal information, or payment.|Guide to recognizing and responding to [[Voice phishing|vishing]] calls]]
{{anchor|Vishing}}
{{Main article|Voice phishing}}
Vishing, or voice phishing, uses telephone or [[Voice over IP]] calls to deliver the lure.<ref name="Thomopoulos2024" /><ref name="vishing">{{cite conference |last1=Griffin |first1=Slade E. |last2=Rackley |first2=Casey C. |title=Vishing |date=September 26, 2008 |book-title=Proceedings of the 5th Annual Conference on Information Security Curriculum Development |series=InfoSecCD '08 |location=New York |publisher=[[Association for Computing Machinery]] |pages=33–35 |doi=10.1145/1456625.1456635 |isbn=978-1-60558-333-4}}</ref> Attackers may make automated calls, use [[Speech synthesis|text-to-speech]], and claim that fraudulent activity has occurred on the recipient's account. They may [[Caller ID spoofing|spoof the caller number]] so that it appears to come from a bank or other institution. The victim is then prompted to enter sensitive information or connected to a person who uses [[Social engineering (security)|social-engineering]] tactics to obtain it.<ref name="vishing" /> A 2008 study found that voice phishing could exploit greater trust in voice [[telephony]] than in email.<ref name="vishingtrust">{{cite conference |last1=Wang |first1=Xinyuan |last2=Zhang |first2=Ruishan |last3=Yang |first3=Xiaohui |last4=Jiang |first4=Xuxian |last5=Wijesekera |first5=Duminda |title=Voice pharming attack and the trust of VoIP |date=September 22, 2008 |book-title=Proceedings of the 4th International Conference on Security and Privacy in Communication Networks |series=SecureComm '08 |publisher=[[Association for Computing Machinery|ACM]] |pages=1–11 |doi=10.1145/1460877.1460908 |isbn=978-1-60558-241-2}}</ref>

===SMS phishing (smishing)===
{{anchor|SMS phishing}}
{{Redirect-distinguish|Smishing|Swishing}}
[[File:Example phishing SMS.svg|thumb|upright=1.1|alt=Fictional parcel-delivery text saying that a delivery failed and asking the recipient to open a link.|Example [[SMS]] phishing message]]
Smishing is phishing delivered through [[SMS]] or [[Multimedia Messaging Service|MMS]], often through an impersonating message, a malicious link, or a malware lure.<ref name="Thomopoulos2024" /> The victim may be asked to click a link, call a phone number, or provide private information such as login credentials.<ref>{{cite web |title=Scam Glossary |url=https://www.fcc.gov/scam-glossary |website=[[Federal Communications Commission]] |access-date=April 29, 2026}}</ref> The limited display of [[URL]]s on [[Mobile device|mobile devices]] can make illegitimate links harder to identify.<ref>{{cite conference |last1=Mishra |first1=Sandhya |last2=Soni |first2=Devpriya |title=SMS Phishing and Mitigation Approaches |book-title=2019 Twelfth International Conference on Contemporary Computing (IC3) |date=August 2019 |pages=1–5 |publisher=[[Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers|IEEE]] |doi=10.1109/IC3.2019.8844920 |isbn=978-1-7281-3591-5 |s2cid=202700726}}</ref>

===QR code phishing (quishing)===
{{anchor|Quishing}}
In "quishing" ([[QR code]] phishing), a code directs the person who scans it to a malicious website. Because QR codes conceal their destination in a form people cannot read directly, users may struggle to distinguish malicious codes from legitimate ones. Across three experiments with 1,876 participants, almost no participants identified QR-based phishing.<ref name="Kowalewski2025">{{cite conference |last1=Kowalewski |first1=Marvin |last2=Lassak |first2=Leona |last3=Dürmuth |first3=Markus |last4=Schnitzler |first4=Theodor |title=Scanned and Scammed: Insecurity by ObsQRity? Measuring User Susceptibility and Awareness of QR Code-Based Attacks |book-title=34th USENIX Security Symposium |publisher=[[USENIX|USENIX Association]] |year=2025 |pages=1415–1434 |url=https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/kowalewski |access-date=September 20, 2026}}</ref> Bogus codes may be sent by email or [[social media]] or printed on stickers placed over legitimate QR codes, such as those on car park notices.<ref name="BBCquish">{{cite news |last=Morris |first=Joanna |date=November 18, 2023 |title=Thornaby: Woman targeted in £13k railway station QR code scam |url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/uk-england-tees-67335952 |work=[[BBC News]] |access-date=November 5, 2024}}</ref> QR phishing can also be combined with a browser-in-the-browser lure to induce the victim to approve the attacker's [[Multi-factor authentication|two-factor access]].<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Akram |first1=Muhammad Wahid |last2=Sood |first2=Keshav |last3=Ul Hassan |first3=Muneeb |last4=Subba |first4=Basant |date=December 2025 |title=Exemplifying Emerging Phishing: QR-Based Browser-in-the-Browser (BiTB) Attack |journal=IEEE Networking Letters |volume=7 |issue=4 |pages=274–278 |doi=10.1109/LNET.2025.3605640 |arxiv=2505.18944 |bibcode=2025INetL...7..274A |issn=2576-3156}}</ref> The UK's [[National Cyber Security Centre (United Kingdom)|National Cyber Security Centre]] assesses QR-code phishing as less common than other types of cyber fraud.<ref name="NCSCquish">{{cite web |last=C |first=David |date=March 11, 2024 |title=QR Codes—what's the real risk? |url=https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blog-post/qr-codes-whats-real-risk |website=National Cyber Security Centre |access-date=November 5, 2024}}</ref>

===Adversary-in-the-middle phishing===
{{anchor|Man-in-the-Middle phishing}}
In adversary-in-the-middle (AiTM) phishing, a victim interacts with an impostor site that relays the [[authentication]] exchange to the legitimate service in real time. Authentication methods that require a person to enter a [[one-time password|one-time code]] are not phishing-resistant because the impostor can relay that code to the real verifier.<ref name="NIST80063B4">{{cite report |last1=Temoshok |first1=David |last2=Fenton |first2=James L. |last3=Choong |first3=Yee-Yin |last4=Lefkovitz |first4=Naomi |last5=Regenscheid |first5=Andrew |last6=Galluzzo |first6=Ryan |last7=Richer |first7=Justin P. |display-authors=3 |title=Digital Identity Guidelines: Authentication and Authenticator Management |series=NIST Special Publication |number=800-63B-4 |date=July 2025 |publisher=[[National Institute of Standards and Technology]] |doi=10.6028/NIST.SP.800-63B-4 |doi-access=free}}</ref> Toolkits such as Evilginx automate this pattern by relaying credentials and a two-factor code while the victim is interacting with the phishing site.<ref name="LinEtAl2022Fingerprinting">{{cite conference |last1=Lin |first1=Xu |last2=Ilia |first2=Panagiotis |last3=Solanki |first3=Saumya |last4=Polakis |first4=Jason |title=Phish in Sheep's Clothing: Exploring the Authentication Pitfalls of Browser Fingerprinting |book-title=31st USENIX Security Symposium |publisher=[[USENIX|USENIX Association]] |year=2022 |pages=1651–1668 |url=https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity22/presentation/lin-xu |access-date=September 20, 2026}}</ref> An authenticated web session is commonly maintained with a [[HTTP cookie|session cookie]]; possession of such a bearer secret can permit use of the session until it expires or is invalidated.<ref name="NIST80063B4" /> Services that use short-lived session cookies restrict how long the captured session can be reused.<ref name="LinEtAl2022Fingerprinting" />

==Techniques==

===Drive-by compromise and phishing===
{{anchor|Page hijacking}}
MITRE ATT&CK classifies drive-by compromise and phishing as separate initial-access techniques.<ref name="MITRET1189" /><ref name="MITRET1566">{{cite web |title=Phishing (T1566) |url=https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1566/ |website=MITRE ATT&CK |publisher=The MITRE Corporation |date=May 12, 2026 |access-date=September 21, 2026}}</ref> In a drive-by compromise, attackers place [[malware|malicious code]] on a website that a victim visits, for example by compromising a legitimate site or [[Code injection|injecting scripts]] or inline frames. If the site is selected because it is frequented by a particular community or organization, the operation is commonly called a [[Watering hole attack|watering-hole attack]].<ref name="MITRET1189">{{cite web |title=Drive-by Compromise (T1189) |url=https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1189/ |website=MITRE ATT&CK |publisher=[[Mitre Corporation|The MITRE Corporation]] |date=October 24, 2025 |access-date=September 20, 2026}}</ref> A phishing message can nevertheless link to a website used for drive-by compromise, so the two techniques can occur in the same campaign.<ref name="MITRET1189" /><ref name="MITRET1566" />

===Link manipulation===
Phishing links may use [[Typosquatting|misspelled registered domains]] or deceptive [[subdomain]]s to conceal their destination.<ref name="Lain2025">{{cite conference |last1=Lain |first1=Daniele |last2=Nakatsuka |first2=Yoshimichi |last3=Kostiainen |first3=Kari |last4=Tsudik |first4=Gene |last5=Capkun |first5=Srdjan |title=URL Inspection Tasks: Helping Users Detect Phishing Links in Emails |book-title=34th USENIX Security Symposium |publisher=[[USENIX|USENIX Association]] |year=2025 |pages=1435–1454 |url=https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/lain |access-date=September 20, 2026}}</ref> In the example {{code|http://www.yourbank.example.com/}}, the registered domain is {{code|example.com}}, while {{code|yourbank}} is only a subdomain. Another tactic is to make the link's displayed text appear trustworthy while the link itself leads to the phisher's site.

[[Internationalized domain name]]s (IDNs) can be exploited via [[Internationalized domain names#ASCII spoofing concerns|IDN spoofing]]<ref>{{cite web |author=Johanson, Eric |title=The State of Homograph Attacks Rev1.1 |work=The Shmoo Group |url=http://www.shmoo.com/idn/homograph.txt |access-date=August 11, 2005 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20050823111335/http://www.shmoo.com/idn/homograph.txt |archive-date=August 23, 2005 }}</ref> or [[IDN homograph attack|homograph attacks]]<ref>{{cite journal |author1=Evgeniy Gabrilovich |author1-link=Evgeniy Gabrilovich |author2=Alex Gontmakher |name-list-style=amp |title=The Homograph Attack |journal=[[Communications of the ACM]] |date=February 2002 |volume=45 |page=128 |url=https://www.cs.technion.ac.il/~gabr/papers/homograph_full.pdf |issue=2 |doi=10.1145/503124.503156 |s2cid=73840 |access-date=September 15, 2019 |archive-date=November 4, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191104181513/http://www.cs.technion.ac.il/~gabr/papers/homograph_full.pdf }}</ref> to allow attackers to create fake websites with visually identical addresses to legitimate ones. Separately, phishers have used open [[URL redirector]]s on trusted websites to disguise malicious destinations.<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.theregister.co.uk/2006/08/15/barclays_phish_scam/ |last=Leyden |first=John |title=Barclays scripting SNAFU exploited by phishers |date=August 15, 2006 |work=[[The Register]] |access-date=August 10, 2017 |archive-date=June 13, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190613055130/https://www.theregister.co.uk/2006/08/15/barclays_phish_scam/ |url-status=live }}</ref><ref>{{cite web |author=Levine, Jason |title=Goin' phishing with eBay |work=Q Daily News |url=http://q.queso.com/archives/001617 |access-date=December 14, 2006 |archive-date=March 26, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190326095522/http://q.queso.com/archives/001617 |url-status=live }}</ref><ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.theregister.co.uk/2007/12/12/phishing_redirection/ |last=Leyden |first=John |title=Cybercrooks lurk in shadows of big-name websites |date=December 12, 2007 |work=[[The Register]] |access-date=August 10, 2017 |archive-date=June 23, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190623125007/https://www.theregister.co.uk/2007/12/12/phishing_redirection/ |url-status=live }}</ref> In <code><nowiki>http://www.exаmple.com/</nowiki></code>, for example, the third character is not the [[Latin script|Latin]] letter '[[a]]' but the [[Cyrillic script|Cyrillic]] character '[[а]]'. A person who follows that link visits the malicious site <code><nowiki>http://www.xn--exmple-4nf.com/</nowiki></code>.

===Social engineering===
[[File:Computer virus scam.jpg|thumb|upright=1.21|alt=Pop-up window claiming that a laptop has a virus and urging the user to install security software.|[[Rogue security software|Fake antivirus]] warning]]
Phishing often uses [[Social engineering (security)|social engineering]] techniques to trick users into performing actions such as clicking a link, opening an [[Email attachment|attachment]], or revealing [[Information sensitivity|sensitive information]]. It often involves impersonating a trusted entity and creating a sense of urgency,<ref name="urgencycues2">{{cite book|last1=Cui|first1=Xinyue|last2=Ge|first2=Yan|last3=Qu|first3=Weina|last4=Zhang|first4=Kan|chapter=Effects of Recipient Information and Urgency Cues on Phishing Detection |date=2020|title=HCI International 2020 - Posters|series=Communications in Computer and Information Science|volume=1226|pages=520–525|doi=10.1007/978-3-030-50732-9_67|isbn=978-3-030-50731-2|s2cid=220523895 }}</ref> like threatening to close or seize a victim's bank or insurance account.<ref name="Williams">{{cite journal|last1=Williams|first1=Emma J|last2=Joinson|first2=Adam N|date=January 1, 2020|title=Developing a measure of information seeking about phishing|journal=Journal of Cybersecurity|volume=6|issue=1|article-number=tyaa001 |doi=10.1093/cybsec/tyaa001|issn=2057-2085|doi-access=free|hdl=1983/7ba801b9-f6b8-4fc1-8393-de5238e76b2f|hdl-access=free}}</ref> Phishing attacks may rely on [[Website spoofing|spoofing]] an organization's website to trick victims into entering credentials.<ref>{{cite web |title=Spoofing and Phishing |url=https://www.fbi.gov/how-we-can-help-you/common-frauds-and-scams/spoofing-and-phishing |archive-url=http://web.archive.org/web/20260910031532/https://www.fbi.gov/how-we-can-help-you/common-frauds-and-scams/spoofing-and-phishing |archive-date=September 10, 2026 |access-date=September 16, 2026 |website=[[Federal Bureau of Investigation]]}}</ref>

[[File:ClickFix Example English.png|thumb|right|upright=1.57|alt=Fake human-verification page instructing the user to paste text into the Windows Run dialog.|Mock [[ClickFix]] prompt posing as human verification]]

[[Generative AI]] can make phishing and other social-engineering content more convincing by producing realistic text and images. It can also reduce the effort needed to personalize and automate attacks.<ref>{{cite journal |last1=Schmitt |first1=Marc |last2=Flechais |first2=Ivan |date=October 12, 2024 |title=Digital deception: generative artificial intelligence in social engineering and phishing |url=https://doi.org/10.1007/s10462-024-10973-2 |journal=Artificial Intelligence Review |volume=57 |issue=12 |article-number=324 |doi=10.1007/s10462-024-10973-2 |issn=1573-7462 |doi-access=free}}</ref><ref name="Czybik2026" />

In a 2026 experiment with 7,700 participants, LLM-personalized emails produced almost three times the click rate of generic phishing emails, at an estimated cost of about {{US$|0.03}} per message.<ref name="Czybik2026" />

==History==
{{main|List of phishing incidents}}

===Early history===
Phishing was documented on [[AOL]] in 1995, and the term appeared in print by March 1997. Its spelling is generally linked to the earlier term "[[phreaking]]".<ref name="Thomopoulos2024" /> AOHell, released in 1994, allowed users to impersonate AOL staff and send [[instant message]]s asking victims to reveal their passwords.<ref>{{cite news |last=Langberg |first=Mike |title=AOL Acts to Thwart Hackers |date=September 8, 1995 |work=[[San Jose Mercury News]] |url=http://simson.net/clips/1995/95.SJMN.AOL_Hackers.html |access-date=March 14, 2012 |archive-date=April 29, 2016 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160429161112/http://simson.net/clips/1995/95.SJMN.AOL_Hackers.html |url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{cite arXiv |last=Rekouche |first=Koceilah |eprint=1106.4692 |title=Early Phishing |year=2011 |class=cs.CR}}</ref>

===2000s===
In the 2000s, phishing attacks became more organized and targeted. The first known direct attempt against a [[payment system]], [[E-gold]], occurred in June 2001, and shortly after the [[September 11 attacks]], a "post-9/11 id check" phishing attack followed.<ref>{{cite web |title=GP4.3 – Growth and Fraud&nbsp;— Case #3 – Phishing |work=Financial Cryptography |url=https://financialcryptography.com/mt/archives/000609.html |date=December 30, 2005 |access-date=February 23, 2007 |archive-date=January 22, 2019 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190122062718/http://financialcryptography.com/mt/archives/000609.html |url-status=live }}</ref> The first known phishing attack against a retail bank was reported in September 2003.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Sangani |first=Kris |title=The Battle Against Identity Theft |journal=The Banker |date=September 2003 |volume=70 |issue=9 |pages=53–54}}</ref> Between May 2004 and May 2005, approximately 1.2 million computer users in the United States suffered losses caused by phishing, totaling approximately {{US$|929 million}}.<ref>{{cite news |url=http://www.csoonline.com/talkback/071905.html ==Anti-phishing==
Anti-phishing programs combine measures at the [[Information security awareness|training]], mail, [[Web browser|browser]], network, [[authentication]], and [[Computer security incident management|incident-response]] layers.<ref name="Jos2007">{{cite web |last=Jøsang |first=Audun |title=Security Usability Principles for Vulnerability Analysis and Risk Assessment |work=Proceedings of the Annual Computer Security Applications Conference 2007 (ACSAC'07) |year=2007 |url=https://www.researchgate.net/publication/27477067 |display-authors=etal |access-date=November 11, 2020 |archive-date=March 21, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210321122454/https://www.researchgate.net/publication/27477067_Security_Usability_Principles_for_Vulnerability_Analysis_and_Risk_Assessment_presented_at |url-status=live}}</ref>

===User training===
[[File:Phish.jpg|thumb|upright=1.21|alt=Cartoon fish warning not to take the bait while a paper asks for a credit-card number.|Frame from a [[Federal Trade Commission]] anti-phishing animation]]

Organizations commonly use simulated-phishing exercises that show training material after a recipient interacts with a test message.<ref name="Lain2024">{{cite conference |last1=Lain |first1=Daniele |last2=Jost |first2=Tarek |last3=Matetić |first3=Siniša |last4=Kostiainen |first4=Kari |last5=Čapkun |first5=Srdjan |date=October 2024 |title=Content, Nudges and Incentives: A Study on the Effectiveness and Perception of Embedded Phishing Training |book-title=Proceedings of the 2024 ACM SIGSAC Conference on Computer and Communications Security |pages=4182–4196 |publisher=[[Association for Computing Machinery]] |doi=10.1145/3658644.3690348 |url=https://syssec.ethz.ch/publications/2024-10-14-content-nudges-and-incentives-a-study-on-the-effectiveness-and-perception-of-embedded-phishing-training-20-500-11850-690712/}}</ref> One 2024 field study of a partner company's employees found that, in that setting, the measured benefit of embedded training came mainly from the periodic reminder created by the exercise rather than from material shown after a click; employees often did not consume that material, and mandatory completion did not improve secure behavior.<ref name="Lain2024" />

===Technical approaches===
Modern technical defenses use overlapping controls rather than relying on a single filter or authentication step.

====Mail filtering and sender authentication====
Mail systems use content and reputation filtering alongside standards that [[Email authentication|authenticate sending domains]].<ref name="2019cfs" /> [[Sender Policy Framework]] (SPF) and [[DomainKeys Identified Mail]] (DKIM) provide domain-level authentication, while [[DMARC]] checks whether a passing SPF or DKIM identifier aligns with the author domain shown in the message's {{code|From}} field and lets a domain owner publish handling and reporting policies.<ref name="RFC9989">{{cite tech report |last1=Herr |first1=Todd M. |last2=Levine |first2=John |date=May 2026 |title=Domain-Based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance (DMARC) |institution=[[Internet Engineering Task Force]] |number=RFC 9989 |doi=10.17487/RFC9989 |url=https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9989.html}}</ref> DMARC addresses only some forms of [[Email spoofing|exact-domain spoofing]]: it does not validate display names, visually similar domains, or message content, and a DMARC pass does not establish that a message is safe.<ref name="RFC9989" /> In a 2021 experiment covering 30 popular mail services and 23 [[Email client|email clients]], every tested service or client was vulnerable to at least one newly identified sender-spoofing technique that exploited inconsistencies in the authentication chain.<ref name="Shen2021">{{cite conference |last1=Shen |first1=Kaiwen |last2=Wang |first2=Chuhan |last3=Guo |first3=Minglei |last4=Zheng |first4=Xiaofeng |last5=Lu |first5=Chaoyi |last6=Liu |first6=Baojun |last7=Zhao |first7=Yuxuan |last8=Hao |first8=Shuang |last9=Duan |first9=Haixin |last10=Pan |first10=Qingfeng |last11=Yang |first11=Min |display-authors=3 |date=August 2021 |title=Weak Links in Authentication Chains: A Large-scale Analysis of Email Sender Spoofing Attacks |book-title=30th USENIX Security Symposium |pages=3201–3217 |publisher=[[USENIX|USENIX Association]] |url=https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity21/presentation/shen-kaiwen}}</ref>

====Browser and network blocking====
Major desktop and mobile browsers display warnings for destinations on [[Blacklist (computing)|anti-phishing blocklists]], sometimes supplemented by [[Heuristic (computer science)|heuristic classifiers]]. These systems are reactive, so there can be a gap between a site's deployment and its addition to a list, and evasive sites may avoid prompt classification.<ref name="Oest2020">{{cite conference |last1=Oest |first1=Adam |last2=Safaei |first2=Yeganeh |last3=Zhang |first3=Penghui |last4=Wardman |first4=Brad |last5=Tyers |first5=Kevin |last6=Shoshitaishvili |first6=Yan |last7=Doupé |first7=Adam |last8=Ahn |first8=Gail-Joon |display-authors=3 |date=August 2020 |title=PhishTime: Continuous Longitudinal Measurement of the Effectiveness of Anti-phishing Blacklists |book-title=29th USENIX Security Symposium |pages=379–396 |publisher=[[USENIX|USENIX Association]] |url=https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity20/presentation/oest-phishtime}}</ref> At the network level, protective [[Domain Name System|DNS]] can refuse to resolve domains identified by phishing [[Cyber threat intelligence|threat feeds]] and can log matching queries, adding an enforcement layer that does not depend on features in each client.<ref name="NISTDNS2026">{{cite report |last1=Rose |first1=Scott |last2=Liu |first2=Cricket |last3=Gibson |first3=Ross |date=March 2026 |title=Secure Domain Name System (DNS) Deployment Guide |publisher=[[National Institute of Standards and Technology]] |number=NIST SP 800-81 Rev. 3 |doi=10.6028/NIST.SP.800-81r3 |url=https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/81/r3/final}}</ref>

====Phishing-resistant authentication and session protection====
[[Multi-factor authentication]] can limit the value of a stolen password, but not every second factor resists phishing. [[National Institute of Standards and Technology|NIST]] does not classify manually entered [[one-time password]]s or out-of-band codes as phishing-resistant because an impostor verifier can relay the value to the real service.<ref name="NIST80063B4" /> [[WebAuthn]] instead binds a cryptographic response to the authenticated relying-party domain; when correctly implemented, syncable WebAuthn authenticators, commonly called [[WebAuthn#Passkey branding|passkeys]], can provide phishing resistance.<ref name="NISTPasskeys">{{cite web |last=Galluzzo |first=Ryan |date=April 22, 2024 |title=Giving NIST Digital Identity Guidelines a Boost: Supplement for Incorporating Syncable Authenticators |url=https://www.nist.gov/blogs/cybersecurity-insights/giving-nist-digital-identity-guidelines-boost-supplement-incorporating |website=Cybersecurity Insights |publisher=[[National Institute of Standards and Technology]] |access-date=September 20, 2026}}</ref>

An [[#Adversary-in-the-middle phishing|adversary-in-the-middle]] proxy can relay a password and a manually entered one-time code during a live login.<ref name="NIST80063B4" /> Authentication may then create a [[Session (computer networking)|browser session]] maintained by a [[HTTP cookie|cookie]] used as a bearer secret; possession of that secret, rather than repeated entry of the user's authenticators, maintains the session.<ref name="NIST80063B4" /> Phishing-resistant sign-in should therefore be paired with controls for the session and its tokens. NIST recommends short, defined lifetimes for identity and [[Access token|access tokens]], revocation and audience restrictions, session monitoring, and sender-constrained or proof-of-possession tokens where feasible.<ref name="NISTIR8587">{{cite report |last1=Galluzzo |first1=Ryan |last2=Regenscheid |first2=Andrew |last3=Nelson |first3=Stephanie |last4=Lazcano |first4=Christine |date=September 2026 |title=Protecting Tokens and Assertions from Forgery, Theft, and Misuse: Implementation Recommendations for Agencies and Cloud Service Providers |publisher=[[National Institute of Standards and Technology]] |number=NIST IR 8587 |doi=10.6028/NIST.IR.8587 |doi-access=free |url=https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ir/8587/final}}</ref>

====Detection, reporting and takedown====
Reports from users and automated detection systems can feed newly discovered [[URL]]s to anti-phishing blocklists. Operators may also ask a [[Web hosting service|hosting provider]] or [[Domain name registrar|domain registrar]] to disable the infrastructure, but takedown depends on cooperation and may take hours or days; blocklisting can act sooner, although it has its own coverage and latency gaps.<ref name="Oest2020" /> A response can therefore combine rapid reporting and blocking with token revocation after a compromise and, where possible, infrastructure takedown.<ref name="Oest2020" /><ref name="NISTIR8587" />

===Legal responses===
Phishing is usually addressed through laws covering the conduct involved rather than through a single offense. In the United States, the [[Computer Fraud and Abuse Act]] applies to specified forms of unauthorized computer access, while other federal provisions may apply to [[Mail and wire fraud#Wire fraud|wire fraud]], access-device fraud, and [[Identity theft|aggravated identity theft]], depending on the facts.<ref name="Berris2023">{{cite report |last=Berris |first=Peter G. |date=May 16, 2023 |title=Cybercrime and the Law: Primer on the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act and Related Statutes |url=https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R47557/R47557.1.pdf |publisher=[[Congressional Research Service]] |id=R47557 |pages=36–39 |access-date=September 20, 2026}}</ref>

In the European Union, Directive (EU) 2019/713 requires member states to criminalize specified conduct involving non-cash payment instruments; its recitals identify phishing and redirection to imitation websites as ways of obtaining payment instruments for fraud.<ref name="EU2019-713">{{cite web |date=April 17, 2019 |title=Directive (EU) 2019/713 of the European Parliament and of the Council on combating fraud and counterfeiting of non-cash means of payment |url=https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32019L0713 |website=[[EUR-Lex]] |publisher=[[European Union]] |at=recital 13 and arts. 1–6 |access-date=September 20, 2026}}</ref> In England and Wales, prosecution guidance treats phishing as a form of [[Cybercrime|cyber-enabled fraud]] and points prosecutors to the [[Fraud Act 2006]], the [[Computer Misuse Act 1990]], and related legislation, with the charge determined by the conduct.<ref name="CPSCybercrime2026">{{cite web |date=June 29, 2026 |title=Cybercrime—prosecution guidance |url=https://www.cps.gov.uk/prosecution-guidance/cybercrime-prosecution-guidance |website=[[Crown Prosecution Service]] |at=sections "Fraud" and "Relevant Offences and Legislation" |access-date=September 20, 2026}}</ref>

Because phishing campaigns, infrastructure, victims, and evidence can be spread across several countries, enforcement also relies on international cooperation. The [[Budapest Convention on Cybercrime|Budapest Convention]] combines common offense categories with powers for obtaining electronic evidence and mechanisms for cooperation among its parties.<ref name="BudapestConvention">{{cite web |title=Benefits of the Convention on Cybercrime (Budapest Convention) |url=https://www.coe.int/en/web/cybercrime/benefits-of-acceding |website=Cybercrime |publisher=[[Council of Europe]] |access-date=September 20, 2026}}</ref> {{As of|2026|9|20}}, the [[United Nations Convention against Cybercrime]] had 82 signatories and three parties but was not yet in force; under Article 65, it will enter into force 90 days after the fortieth ratification, acceptance, approval, or accession.<ref name="UNCybercrimeTreaty">{{cite web |date=December 24, 2024 |title=United Nations Convention against Cybercrime; Strengthening International Cooperation for Combating Certain Crimes Committed by Means of Information and Communications Technology Systems and for the Sharing of Evidence in Electronic Form of Serious Crimes |url=https://treaties.un.org/Pages/ViewDetails.aspx?chapter=18&mtdsg_no=XVIII-16&src=TREATY |website=United Nations Treaty Collection |publisher=[[United Nations]] |at=status as at September 20, 2026; art. 65(1) |access-date=September 20, 2026}}</ref>
